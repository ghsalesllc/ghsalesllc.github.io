Finishing guide

How to finish redwood, inside and out

Redwood boards, posts and tongue and groove work outdoors on fences, decks, siding, pergolas, gates, planters and furniture, and indoors on walls, ceilings, mantels and tables. Outdoors, the job is protecting the wood from sun and water, and managing redwood's natural tannins. Indoors, it's mostly about the look you want and keeping it easy to clean.

Outdoors

Protecting redwood from sun, water and tannin stains

Sunlight starts breaking down the surface of bare wood within days, and water that soaks in (especially through cut ends) leads to swelling, cupping, stains and eventually decay. A good finish slows all of that down. Redwood heartwood already has natural extractives that help it resist decay and insects, but those same water-soluble tannins can leave reddish-brown stains as they wash out, and they react with plain steel fasteners to leave dark streaks. There's no single right finish; it depends on the look you want and how much upkeep you're willing to do. The comparison table below lays out the options.

Outdoors: before you install

  • Let it acclimate. Store the boards flat, dry and covered at the job site so they adjust to local humidity. If the wood feels damp or has been sitting in the weather, let it dry before finishing. Finishing wet wood can blister, peel or stain.
  • Finish all sides before installing. Coat the back, edges, ends and (on tongue and groove) the tongue and groove, not just the face. That evens out moisture movement, reduces cupping, keeps bare stripes from showing when boards shrink, and cuts down on tannin bleed onto joists, concrete or siding below.
  • Seal every cut end. End grain soaks up water up to 100 times faster than the face. Brush finish or primer on every cut before the board goes up; end grain may need two coats. This matters most at the bottoms of fence boards, posts, gate rails and planter sides.
  • Finish soon. Softwoods start to "sunburn" fast, and even a short stretch of bare sun can shorten the life of a later paint or solid-stain coat. If you can, finish before installing and touch up afterward.
  • Use stainless steel, aluminum or hot-dip galvanized fasteners. Redwood's tannins react with plain steel and with thin electroplated coatings, leaving blue-black or dark gray streaks around nails and screws. Don't clean it with steel wool or a wire brush for the same reason; use a stiff non-metal brush instead.
  • Test on a scrap first. Heartwood and sapwood can take stain a little differently, and oils darken redwood. Try your finish on a spare piece and let it dry before you commit, and stick with one product (and one batch, if you can) for the whole job.
  • Follow the label. Every product has its own temperature, dry-time and coverage directions. Many latex paints need 50°F or warmer for at least 24 hours, and most finishes shouldn't go on in hot direct sun or just before rain. A calm day in the 50s to 70s is a good window.
  • Help it dry out. Roof overhangs, flashing, a gap between siding or fence boards and the ground, and a rain screen behind siding all help any finish last longer. Keep sprinklers from hitting the wood every day.

General ranges from wood-finishing research and finish makers' guidance. Actual life depends on the product, how it's applied, sun, weather and upkeep.

1. Penetrating exterior oil

Choose an oil made for exterior wood, ideally tinted or with UV and mildew protection, and wipe off whatever doesn't soak in so it doesn't stay sticky. Plain natural oils, including linseed oil used alone, can feed mildew and hold dirt. Oily rags can catch fire on their own: lay them flat to dry or seal them in a water-filled metal can.

2. Semi-transparent penetrating stain

Brush it in (or spray and back-brush), work two or three boards at a time along their full length to avoid lap marks, and wipe off anything still shiny after an hour so it doesn't form a film. Water-based "semi-transparent" stains often form a thin film that can flake, so read the label.

3. Clear water repellent or transparent toner

Recoated on schedule, these can hold a warm buckskin tan instead of letting the wood gray. They're easy to reapply, including on end grain, but they have little sun protection, so they last about 1 to 2 years in full weather (sometimes 12 to 18 months in damp climates). Easiest to live with on covered soffits and porch ceilings, or if you're willing to recoat often.

4. Solid (opaque) stain

Use a stain-blocking primer first so redwood's tannins don't bleed through, then two coats; one coat on smooth bare wood tends to crack and flake. Acrylic latex solid stains generally perform best. Not made for walking surfaces like decks.

5. Paint (primer plus two topcoats)

Prime all sides if you can, with a stain-blocking primer made to hold back extractives, then add two coats of quality exterior acrylic latex, or at least two on the sunny south and west sides. More coats of the same topcoat won't stop tannin bleed if the primer didn't. If it peels later, scrape and sand before repainting.

6. Clear varnish and spar urethane: why we don't recommend them outside

Sunlight passes through a clear film and breaks down the wood underneath, so the finish often cracks and peels within a year or two in full sun. Refinishing means sanding or stripping it off. Spar varnish made for boats holds up better than most, but still needs frequent upkeep. For a clear look outdoors, a penetrating oil or clear water repellent is easier to live with.

7. Let it weather to gray

Left bare, redwood usually darkens first, then gradually weathers to a soft driftwood gray, often silvery in dry climates and darker or blotchy where it stays damp. Early on, tannins can wash out and stain concrete, stucco or wood below; that usually eases after the first rainy season. A weathering stain evens out the gray. If you paint or solid-stain later, clean and often brighten or sand back to fresh wood first.

Fences and gates

Finishing a redwood fence

Fence boards, including 5/4 boards and 1x4 dog-eared pickets, get sun and rain on both faces plus splash and moisture at the bottom. Finish a gate the same way, and pay special attention to the bottom edge, which soaks up splash, and to hinges, latches and fasteners.

  • Use a penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain on all sides and cut ends. A clear water repellent works if you want to keep the natural color and will recoat more often. Solid stain or paint over a stain-blocking primer lasts longer between coats but hides the grain. Skip clear varnish and spar urethane outside. See the outdoor finish options for a full comparison, including leaving it unfinished.
  • Finish before you build if you can. Coating all four sides and both ends protects the faces you can't reach later, cuts down on cupping and checking, and reduces tannin bleed onto posts and concrete.
  • Seal every cut end, especially the bottoms, which soak up water fastest. Leave a gap of an inch or two above the soil, and keep sprinklers from hitting the fence every day.
  • Use stainless, aluminum or hot-dip galvanized fasteners and hardware. Redwood's tannins can react with plain steel and leave dark streaks on pickets, rails and gates.
  • Recoat every few years when water stops beading or the color fades, starting with the sunny south and west sides and the tops of rails and gates. Or let it weather to a soft gray and wash off dirt and mildew now and then.

Decks

Finishing a redwood deck

Decks are the toughest spot for any finish: direct sun, standing water, snow and foot traffic. Paint, solid stain and varnish tend to peel here, and refinishing means scraping and sanding. Penetrating finishes are the usual choice.

  • Use a penetrating deck oil or semi-transparent penetrating stain made for walking surfaces. Brush it in and wipe off whatever hasn't soaked in so it doesn't leave a sticky film. Rollers and sprayers tend to put down too much on flat surfaces.
  • Plan to recoat about every 1 to 3 years, sooner in full sun. Clean first; no sanding needed.
  • Finish all sides and seal cut ends before the boards go down. Prefinishing the bottoms and edges cuts down on cupping and on tannin stains dripping onto the joists. Use stainless or hot-dip galvanized screws. Leave a gap between boards so water can drain and the wood can dry, and don't let leaves and debris sit in the gaps.
  • Or let it weather to a natural gray, and keep it clean. Unfinished redwood decks often darken first, then bleach to a soft gray.

Siding, pergolas and more

Siding, soffits, pergolas, planters, furniture and posts

Siding, soffits and porch ceilings

Soffits and porch ceilings are shaded and stay drier, so oils, semi-transparent stains and even clear water repellents often last much longer there than on siding. Wash them before recoating; those spots collect dust, and dew can lead to mildew. Sun-facing south and west siding takes the most wear; a semi-transparent stain is the usual pick for a natural look in full sun. If you paint or use a solid stain, prime all sides with a stain-blocking primer so tannins don't bleed through. 1x6 redwood tongue and groove works for siding, soffits, porch ceilings, and interior walls and ceilings.

Pergolas, arbors and garden shelters

The tops of beams and rafters get the most sun and standing water, so they wear like a deck even when the sides wear like siding. Use a penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain on all sides, seal every cut end (including the tops and ends of beams), and use stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners and hardware. Recoat the tops first, often every 1 to 3 years, and the sides when the color fades or water stops beading.

Gates

Finish a gate the same way as a fence: all sides and both ends, especially the bottom edge. Use stainless or hot-dip galvanized hinges, latches and fasteners, and recoat the top rail and the sunny face first.

Planters and garden beds

Soil holds moisture against the wood, so planters stay wetter than fences or siding. Finish all sides and every cut end before you assemble the box, including the inside faces and the bottom edges. Use a penetrating exterior oil or a clear water repellent; film finishes tend to peel where the wood stays damp. Give the box drainage so water doesn't sit against the boards, and keep soil from burying unfinished end grain. Or leave it unfinished and let it weather, knowing you'll see more movement and earlier graying where the wood stays wet.

Outdoor furniture and benches

A penetrating exterior oil or furniture oil made for outdoor wood is the usual choice: it soaks in, shows the grain and is easy to renew. Wipe off the excess so the surface doesn't stay sticky, and re-oil when the wood looks dry, often once a year in full weather. Skip clear varnish outdoors unless the piece lives under a deep cover and you're prepared to refinish when it cracks. 5/4x6 and 2x6 redwood boards, plus 4x4x10 posts, are a common starting point for benches and outdoor builds.

Posts

Posts set in the ground or in concrete take the most moisture of any part of a fence, pergola or deck. Seal all four sides and both ends before the post goes in, and pay special attention to the bottom end. Slope the concrete or soil away from the post so water drains off, and use stainless or hot-dip galvanized hardware where the post meets rails or beams. We carry 4x4x10 redwood posts.

Keeping it looking good

  • Check once a year. If water no longer beads up or the color has faded, it's time to recoat. You don't have to do every wall, rail or deck board at once.
  • Clean gently. Rinse off dirt and pollen first. For built-up grime, use a soft or stiff non-metal brush with water and mild soap, or a wood cleaner made for the finish. Don't power wash; it tears up soft wood fibers. Don't use steel wool or a wire brush, which can leave iron stains.
  • Mildew is common in shady, damp spots and on soffits. Use a commercial mildew cleaner or a wood cleaner made for outdoor wood, rinse well and let it dry before recoating.
  • Tannin (extractive) stains: reddish-brown streaking can show up in the first year, especially after rain, as natural tannins wash out. It is usually cosmetic and often fades with more rain. A wood cleaner or brightener with oxalic acid removes it from the wood, and sometimes from stained concrete below. Prefinishing all sides is the best way to reduce it on a new build.
  • Dark streaks around fasteners that look blue-black or gray are usually iron reacting with redwood's tannins, from plain steel nails or screws, thin electroplated coatings, or steel wool. A brightener with oxalic acid can fade the stain, but it may come back if the iron is still there. The lasting fix is stainless, aluminum or hot-dip galvanized fasteners.
  • Brightening weathered wood. If the surface has gone dull or gray and you want the color back before recoating, use a wood brightener (many contain oxalic acid), rinse well and let the wood dry thoroughly. Follow the cleaner's label.
  • Recoat with the same type of finish. Oil-based stains won't soak into a film finish, and old latex coatings should be refinished with latex.
Indoors

Finishing redwood on walls, ceilings, mantels and furniture

Indoors, redwood doesn't face rain, so finishes last far longer, often for decades. Leaving it bare is fine in dry rooms; a finish mainly makes it easier to wipe clean and protects against smudges and scuffs. On tables and other furniture, a finish also protects against water rings and wear.

Good to know: redwood changes color with light, and tannins can still show up indoors. All wood changes color indoors, finished or not, mostly from ordinary light. Redwood often deepens and mellows over time. Hang art and mount shelves after a couple of months so you don't get "shadow" outlines; if one shows up, leave the area uncovered and it usually evens out over several months. Clear finishes with UV blockers can slow the change near sunny windows, but no clear finish stops it. If you paint redwood in a humid room, use a stain-blocking primer so extractives don't discolor the paint.

Prep for any interior finish

  • Acclimate the boards in the room, and test your finish on a scrap. Oils and oil-based clears darken and enrich redwood. Use the same product throughout. Don't use steel wool when you sand or clean.
  • Sand lightly if needed. To sand out marks or even out a stain, a light pass with fine paper (about 120 to 180 grit) with the grain is plenty. Remove all dust before finishing.
  • Finish the tongues before installing if you're staining or painting tongue and groove, so bare stripes don't show when boards shrink in dry winter air.
  • Ventilate while finishing and curing, especially with oil-based products. Water-based finishes have much lower odor and can raise the grain slightly on the first coat; a light sanding after it dries smooths it out. Follow the label. Oily rags can catch fire on their own: lay them flat outdoors to dry or seal them in a water-filled metal can.

Room by room, and furniture

  • Living rooms, bedrooms and accent walls: unfinished or a matte water-based clear are the most common for a natural look.
  • Ceilings: they rarely get touched, so unfinished, wax or a single matte clear coat is often enough. Finishing boards before they go up saves a lot of overhead work.
  • Wainscoting and hallways: these get bumped and touched, so a durable water-based polyurethane or paint makes cleanup easier.
  • Mantels and beams: unfinished, oil, hardwax oil or a clear coat all work. Oils and oil-based clears bring out the red. If the mantel sits close to a heat source, follow the finish maker's directions.
  • Tables and indoor furniture: use a durable clear (water-based or oil-based polyurethane) or a hardwax oil. Film finishes handle spills and wear better than wax alone. Test the sheen and color on a scrap, and let oils cure fully before heavy use.
  • Bathrooms and laundry rooms: seal all sides and every cut end before installing, use a moisture-resistant finish such as a durable polyurethane (an extra coat helps), and make sure the room has a working vent fan. Keep redwood out of the direct shower spray and away from standing water.

Redwood finishing FAQ

Should I oil, stain or clear-coat redwood?

Outdoors, oil or a semi-transparent stain. Penetrating oils and semi-transparent stains soak in and are renewed with a cleaning and a fresh coat, with no sanding. A clear water repellent can hold a natural buckskin-tan look if you recoat about every 1 to 2 years. Clear film finishes like varnish and spar urethane tend to crack and peel in the sun. Indoors, either works: a water-based clear coat keeps redwood close to its current color, while oils give a richer, darker look.

What's the best finish for a redwood fence?

A penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain on all sides and cut ends, applied before building if you can. It soaks in, shows the grain, and is renewed every few years by cleaning and recoating with no sanding. A clear water repellent also works if you will recoat more often. Or leave it unfinished and let it weather to gray.

What's the best finish for a redwood deck?

A penetrating deck oil or semi-transparent penetrating stain made for walking surfaces. Brush it in, wipe off the excess, seal cut ends, and plan to recoat every 1 to 3 years. Skip paint, solid stain and varnish on decks, because they tend to peel under foot traffic. Or let the deck weather to gray and keep it clean.

Can I leave redwood unfinished?

Yes. Indoors in dry rooms, unfinished redwood is common and you can add a finish later. Outdoors, unfinished redwood usually darkens first, then weathers to a soft driftwood gray. Keep it clean, and a weathering stain can even out the gray.

Why is my redwood getting reddish stains or dark streaks?

Reddish-brown stains are usually tannins (natural extractives) washing out, especially in the first rainy season. They are typically cosmetic and often fade with more rain; a wood brightener with oxalic acid can remove them. Dark blue-black or gray streaks around nails or screws are usually iron reacting with those tannins. Use stainless, aluminum or hot-dip galvanized fasteners, not plain steel, and don't clean the wood with steel wool or a wire brush.

How often do I need to refinish outdoor redwood?

It depends on the finish and how much sun it gets. Penetrating oils typically last about 2 to 5 years on walls, semi-transparent stains about 2 to 6, clear water repellents about 1 to 2, and decks about 1 to 3. Covered soffits and porch ceilings last longer than siding, fences or pergola tops in full sun.

How do I clean and brighten weathered redwood?

Rinse off dirt, then scrub gently with a non-metal brush and mild soap or a wood cleaner. Don't power wash, and don't use steel wool or a wire brush. For grayed wood, tannin stains or iron streaks, use a wood brightener (many contain oxalic acid), rinse well and let the wood dry before recoating. Follow the cleaner's label.

What's the best finish for indoor redwood walls, ceilings or furniture?

In dry rooms, leaving walls, ceilings and mantels unfinished is fine. For a natural look that wipes clean, use a non-yellowing water-based clear coat in matte or satin. Oils and oil-based clears enrich and darken the red. For tables and other furniture, use a durable clear or a hardwax oil so spills and wear wipe off more easily.
