How to Finish Cedar (Japanese Sugi), Inside and Out

Fences, decks, siding, soffits, walls, ceilings and saunas: oil, stain, clear coat or leave it natural, and how to keep it looking good.

Quick answer
  • Outdoors (soffits, siding, porch ceilings): use a penetrating exterior oil or a semi-transparent stain, or let it weather to gray. Skip clear varnish and spar urethane outside, because they tend to crack and peel within a year or two in the sun.
  • Fences, gates and other outdoor builds: use a penetrating oil or semi-transparent stain on all sides and cut ends, keep boards off the ground, and recoat every few years, or let it weather to gray.
  • Decks: use a penetrating deck oil or semi-transparent stain and recoat every 1 to 3 years, or let it weather.
  • Indoors (walls, ceilings, wainscoting): leaving it unfinished is fine in dry rooms. For a natural look that wipes clean, use a non-yellowing water-based clear coat in matte or satin. Oils and oil-based clears look great but darken Sugi.
  • Sauna hot room: leave walls and ceilings bare. Use only a sauna oil or paraffin oil on benches, and never a film finish.
Finishing guide

How to finish Sugi, inside and out

Japanese Sugi tongue and groove and boards work for soffits, porch ceilings, siding and decks outside, and for walls, ceilings, wainscoting and saunas inside. The right finish depends on where the wood goes. Outdoors, the job is protecting it from sun and water. Indoors, it's mostly about the look you want and keeping it easy to clean. Saunas follow their own rules.

Outdoors

Protecting Sugi on soffits, porch ceilings and siding

Like any wood used outdoors, Sugi needs protection from sun and water. Sunlight starts breaking down the surface of bare wood within days, and water that soaks in (especially through cut ends) leads to swelling, cupping, stains and eventually decay. A good finish slows all of that down. There's no single "right" finish; it depends on the look you want and how much upkeep you're willing to do.

Quick picks
  • Natural wood look on a covered soffit or porch ceiling: a penetrating exterior oil, a semi-transparent stain, or even a clear water repellent works well, because these spots get little direct sun.
  • Natural look on sun-facing siding: a semi-transparent penetrating stain. Its pigment blocks the sun better than clear finishes. Plan to recoat every few years.
  • Longest life, least upkeep: a stain-blocking primer plus two coats of quality exterior acrylic latex paint or solid stain.
  • Silver-gray look: let it weather naturally, or use a weathering stain for a more even gray.
  • Skip it outdoors: clear varnish, spar urethane and other clear film finishes on sun-exposed wood.

Outdoors: before you install

  • Let it acclimate. Store the boards flat, dry and covered at the job site before installing so they adjust to local humidity. Finishing wet wood raises the risk of blistering, peeling and stains.
  • Finish all sides before installing. Coat the back, edges, tongue and groove, not just the face. Sealing all sides evens out moisture movement, which reduces cupping, and it keeps bare stripes from showing when boards shrink in dry weather.
  • Seal every cut end. End grain soaks up water up to 100 times faster than the face. Brush finish or primer on every cut before the board goes up; end grain may need two coats.
  • Finish soon after installing. Soft, light woods like Sugi and cedar start to "sunburn" fast. Research on cedar found that even one week of sun before painting shortened paint life. If you can, finish before installing and touch up afterward.
  • Use stainless steel or hot-dip galvanized fasteners. Like other cedar-type woods, Sugi contains tannins that react with plain steel and leave black streaks around nails. Don't clean it with steel wool or a wire brush for the same reason.
  • Test on a scrap first. Sugi's cream sapwood and reddish heartwood take stain differently, so try your finish on a spare piece and let it dry before you commit. Stick with one product (and one batch, if you can) for the whole job.
  • Follow the label. Every product has its own temperature, dry-time and coverage directions. Many latex paints need 50°F or warmer for at least 24 hours, and most finishes shouldn't go on in hot direct sun or just before rain.
  • Help it dry out. Good roof overhangs, flashing, a gap between siding and the ground (about 6 in.) or roof (about 2 in.), and a rain screen behind siding all help any finish last longer.

General ranges from wood-finishing research and finish makers' guidance. Actual life depends on the product, how it's applied, sun, weather and upkeep.

1. Penetrating exterior oil

Exterior wood oils (often linseed or tung-oil based, including exterior "hybrid" or hardwax oils) soak into the wood instead of forming a skin on top. They bring out Sugi's color and grain with a natural, low-sheen look.

  • Pros: they wear away gradually instead of peeling, so recoating is just clean, let dry and re-oil. No sanding.
  • Cons: more frequent upkeep than paint, usually every 2 to 5 years on walls. Clear oils with no pigment or UV protection gray faster.
  • Tips: choose an oil made for exterior wood, ideally tinted or with UV protection. Wipe off whatever doesn't soak in so it doesn't stay sticky. Plain natural oils can feed mildew, so exterior formulas with mildew protection are a better pick. Oily rags can catch fire on their own: lay them flat to dry or seal them in a water-filled metal can.

2. Semi-transparent penetrating stain

A semi-transparent stain adds color, from natural cedar tones to grays like the soffit in our photos, while letting the grain show. For wood fully exposed to the weather, a penetrating (usually oil-based) semi-transparent stain is the cedar industry's top pick when you want a natural look.

  • Pros: more pigment means more sun protection than clear finishes. It fades rather than peels, and each recoat tends to last longer than the one before.
  • Cons: one coat on new, smooth wood may last only 2 to 4 years in full sun. Water-based "semi-transparent" stains often form a thin film that can flake, so read the label.
  • Tips: brush it in (or spray and back-brush). Stain two or three boards at a time along their full length to avoid lap marks. If it still looks shiny an hour later, wipe off the excess so it doesn't form a film.

3. Clear water repellent or transparent toner

Clear water-repellent finishes (often with a mildewcide, sometimes called water-repellent preservatives) and lightly tinted toners keep the look closest to fresh wood.

  • Pros: very natural look, easy to reapply, and great for sealing end grain.
  • Cons: little or no pigment means little sun protection, so they last about 1 to 3 years. They're best on covered soffits and porch ceilings, not sun-facing walls.

4. Solid (opaque) stain

Solid stain covers the wood's color like paint but leaves more of the texture showing. It's a good middle ground between stain and paint.

  • Pros: any color, good protection, often 6 to 10 years or more.
  • Cons: it forms a film, so it can peel if moisture gets behind it, and it's not made for walking surfaces like decks.
  • Tips: use a stain-blocking primer first so tannins don't bleed through, then two coats. One coat straight onto smooth bare wood tends to crack and flake. Acrylic latex solid stains generally perform best.

5. Paint (primer plus two topcoats)

Paint gives the most protection from sun and water and lasts the longest. Light, soft woods like Sugi hold paint well.

  • Pros: a quality primer and two topcoats can last about 10 to 20 years. When it wears thin, washing and adding a fresh topcoat is often all it needs.
  • Cons: hides the grain. If it peels, you'll need to scrape and sand before repainting.
  • Tips: prime (all sides, if you can) with a stain-blocking primer within days of installing, then add two coats of quality exterior acrylic latex. If two topcoats everywhere isn't practical, give the sunny south and west sides two.

6. Clear varnish and spar urethane: why we don't recommend them outside

Glossy clear finishes look great at first, but sunlight passes right through the clear film and breaks down the wood underneath. The film loses its grip, cracks and peels, often within a year or two in full sun, and refinishing means sanding or stripping it all off. Spar varnish made for boats holds up better than most, but still needs frequent upkeep. For a clear look outdoors, a penetrating oil or clear water repellent is easier to live with.

7. Yakisugi (shou sugi ban): charred Sugi

Yakisugi is the traditional Japanese method of charring the surface of Sugi boards, and Sugi is the classic wood for it. The charred layer helps block sun and shed water. Fully charred boards can keep their black finish for decades with little or no upkeep, and many owners re-oil every 5 to 10 years to keep the color rich. Brushed styles, where some char is removed to show the grain, weather like other oiled wood and need oiling more often.

  • Care: clean gently with water and a soft brush or cloth. Never sand, scrape or power wash charred wood.
  • Good to know: our Sugi is sold unfinished, not charred. If you want to try charring it yourself, practice on scraps, work outdoors away from anything that can burn, and keep water or an extinguisher on hand.

8. Let it weather to gray

Left bare, Sugi will gradually turn gray. Cedar-type woods tend to weather to a silvery gray in dry climates and can look darker and blotchy where they stay damp.

  • Pros: nothing to peel and nothing to buy. The graying is only skin deep; weathering wears away roughly 1/4 in. of wood per century.
  • Cons: color is often uneven, with darker wood under eaves and grayer wood in the sun. Expect some surface checking and mildew in shady, damp spots. Sugi's cream sapwood is less decay resistant than its heartwood, so good drainage and airflow matter more without a finish.
  • For an even gray: weathering or bleaching stains (pigmented water-repellent finishes) give a uniform gray sooner and are reapplied from time to time.
  • Changing your mind later: weathered wood must be cleaned and sanded back to fresh wood before paint or solid stain will stick well.

Soffits vs. siding

Soffits and porch ceilings are shaded and stay drier, so finishes there often last much longer than on siding. Paint on a shaded side of a house can last about twice as long as on a sunny side. That's why lighter-duty finishes like oils, semi-transparent stains and even clear water repellents work well on soffits.

The catch: protected spots collect dust, and dew forms on soffits and porch ceilings on cool nights, which can lead to mildew. Wash them before recoating. Sun-facing siding, especially on south and west walls, takes the most wear, so plan on recoating those walls first.

Keeping it looking good

  • Check once a year. If water no longer beads up or the color has faded, it's time to recoat. You don't have to do every wall at once.
  • Clean gently. Use a soft brush with water and mild soap or a wood cleaner made for the finish. Don't power wash; it tears up soft wood fibers.
  • Mildew: use a commercial mildew cleaner or a dilute household bleach and detergent solution, rinse well and let it dry before recoating.
  • Tannin stains: some reddish-brown streaking can show up in the first year as natural tannins wash out. It usually fades with rain; a wood cleaner with oxalic acid removes it.
  • Recoat with the same type of finish. Oil-based stains won't soak into wood that already has a film finish, and old latex coatings should be refinished with latex.
Fences and outdoor builds

Finishing a cedar fence

Fence boards, including 5/4 boards, get sun and rain on both faces plus splash and moisture at the bottom, so they're treated like siding with extra attention to the ends. The same goes for gates, pergolas, screens, trellises and other outdoor builds.

  • Use a penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain. These soak in, let the grain show, and are renewed by cleaning and recoating with no sanding. Solid stain or paint over primer lasts longer between coats but hides the grain. Skip clear varnish and spar urethane outside, since they crack and peel in the sun.
  • Finish before you build if you can. Coating all four sides and both ends of each board first protects the faces you can't reach later and cuts down on cupping and checking.
  • Seal every cut end, especially the bottom ends, which soak up water fastest.
  • Keep boards off the soil. Leave a gap of an inch or two between the bottom of the boards and the ground, and keep sprinklers from hitting the fence every day.
  • Use stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners. Cedar's natural oils can react with plain steel and leave dark streaks.
  • Posts set in the ground take the most moisture. Use posts rated for ground contact, or follow your post maker's directions, and slope the concrete or soil away from the post so water drains off.
  • Recoat every few years. The sunny south and west sides usually need it first. When water stops beading or the color fades, clean and recoat.
  • Or let it weather. Unfinished cedar fences turn a silver gray over time. Wash off dirt and mildew now and then.
Decks

Finishing a Sugi deck

Decks are the toughest spot for any finish: direct sun, standing water, snow and foot traffic. Film finishes like paint, solid stain and varnish tend to peel on decks, and refinishing them means scraping and sanding. Penetrating finishes are the usual choice.

  • Use a penetrating deck oil or semi-transparent penetrating stain. Brush it in, and wipe off whatever hasn't soaked in so it doesn't leave a sticky film. Rollers and sprayers tend to put down too much on flat surfaces.
  • Plan to recoat about every 1 to 3 years, sooner on decks in full sun. Clean first; no sanding needed.
  • Seal cut ends before the boards go down, and use stainless or hot-dip galvanized screws.
  • Or let it weather to a natural gray, and keep it clean.

Indoors

Finishing Sugi on walls, ceilings and wainscoting

Indoors, Sugi doesn't face sun or rain, so finishes last far longer, often for decades. The choice is mostly about looks and cleaning. Leaving it bare is perfectly fine in dry rooms; a finish mainly makes it easier to wipe clean and protects against smudges and scuffs.

Good to know: Sugi changes color with light. All wood changes color indoors, finished or not, mostly from ordinary light. Light woods tend to darken and mellow over time, and Sugi's cream and reddish stripes will blend somewhat. Hang art and mount shelves after a couple of months so you don't get "shadow" outlines; if one shows up, leave the area uncovered and it usually evens out over several months. Clear finishes with UV blockers can slow color change near sunny windows, but no clear finish stops it.

Prep for any interior finish

  • Acclimate first. Bring the boards inside to the room where they'll go and let them adjust before installing.
  • Sand lightly if needed. Our tongue and groove is planed smooth. If you sand out marks or want a more even stain, a light pass with fine paper (about 120 to 180 grit) with the grain is plenty. Remove all dust before finishing.
  • Finish the tongues before installing if you're staining or painting, so bare stripes don't show when boards shrink in dry winter air.
  • Test on a scrap. Sugi's cream sapwood and reddish heartwood can take color very differently, and oils and oil-based clears darken it. Try your finish on a spare piece first, and use the same product throughout.
  • Ventilate. Open windows and run fans while finishing and curing, especially with oil-based products. Water-based finishes have much lower odor. Follow the label for dry and cure times.
  • Water-based finishes can raise the grain slightly on the first coat. A light sanding with fine paper after it dries smooths it out.
  • Oily rags can catch fire on their own. Lay them flat outdoors to dry or seal them in a water-filled metal can.

  • Unfinished: the simplest choice for walls and ceilings in dry rooms. The wood keeps its natural feel, and you can always add a finish later.
  • Water-based clear polyurethane or acrylic: the most popular way to keep Sugi close to its natural color. Choose a non-yellowing formula. Matte and satin look the most like bare wood; gloss is the most durable but shows every bump.
  • Oil-based polyurethane or varnish: tough and easy to clean, but it adds an amber tone that deepens with age. Great if you like a warmer look, not if you want Sugi to stay light.
  • Penetrating oils (tung, boiled linseed, Danish oil): easy to wipe on and give a rich, hand-rubbed look, but they noticeably darken Sugi. Wipe off all excess; these can take a while to fully cure.
  • Hardwax oils: oil-and-wax blends that soak in and leave a natural matte or satin surface. Worn spots can be touched up without sanding the whole wall.
  • Paste or liquid wax: gives smooth wood a soft luster with very little change in color, but offers the least protection from water and stains. Best for walls and ceilings that won't get touched much.
  • Clear lacquer: dries fast and looks unobtrusive on smooth wood. It's usually sprayed in two or more coats and isn't recommended for kitchens, bathrooms or surfaces that get scrubbed.
  • Interior stain: adds color while showing the grain. Softwoods can stain unevenly, so a pre-stain conditioner and a test board help. Topcoat with a clear finish.
  • Whitewash or pickling: a white or white-tinted stain (or thinned paint) brushed on and wiped back for a light, Scandinavian look that also hides Sugi's color contrast. Seal it with a water-based clear coat to keep it from yellowing.
  • Paint: use a stain-blocking primer first, since natural extractives in cedar-type woods can bleed through paint (especially in humid rooms), then two coats of paint. The V-groove or nickel-gap lines still show through.

Room by room

  • Living rooms, bedrooms and accent walls: any of the options above. For a natural look, unfinished or a matte water-based clear are the most common.
  • Ceilings and vaulted ceilings: they rarely get touched, so unfinished, wax or a single matte clear coat is often enough. Finishing boards before they go up saves a lot of overhead work.
  • Wainscoting and hallways: these get bumped and touched, so a durable clear (water-based polyurethane) or paint makes cleanup easier.
  • Closets: many people leave closet linings unfinished so the wood's mild, natural scent comes through.
  • Bathrooms and laundry rooms: moisture is the enemy. Seal all sides and every cut end before installing, use a moisture-resistant finish such as a durable polyurethane (an extra coat helps) or an exterior-grade finish, and make sure the room has a working vent fan. Keep Sugi out of the direct shower spray and away from standing water; wood behind or above a tub or shower should be well sealed and able to dry.
Saunas

Finishing a Sugi sauna

A sauna's hot room is different from any other room. Heat can make ordinary finishes soften, turn sticky or give off fumes, and a film on the wood keeps it from breathing with each heat-up and cool-down. That's why sauna makers recommend keeping finishes to a minimum.

  • Walls and ceilings: usually left unfinished. Many traditional saunas never get any treatment, and some sauna makers say the walls and ceiling shouldn't be treated at all. If you want them easier to clean, use only a product made and labeled for sauna interiors, such as a sauna wax.
  • Benches and backrests: never use a film finish. If you treat them, sauna makers recommend a paraffin oil or sauna oil made for benches. Treat both sides and the ends well, let it soak in for 30 to 60 minutes and wipe off the excess. It darkens the wood slightly, so test it on the underside of a bench first. Renew it about once or twice a year, or when water soaks straight in instead of beading.
  • Skip these in the hot room: varnish, polyurethane, lacquer, paint, deck or exterior stains, regular interior stains and household wood oils with drying agents or solvents.
  • Wet spots: pay extra attention to areas that stay damp, like lower boards next to glass where condensation forms and the floor area near the bucket. Use the sauna maker's recommended treatment there.
  • Daily care beats any finish: sit on a towel, wipe up water and spills, and leave the vent and door open after each session so the wood dries out. Clean with a mild sauna cleaner a few times a year; a light sanding freshens stained benches.
  • The outside of the sauna is a different story: outdoor sauna walls, trailers and eaves need an exterior finish. See the outdoor section above.

Sugi finishing FAQ

Should I oil or clear-coat Sugi?

Outdoors, oil. Penetrating oils and semi-transparent stains soak in and are renewed with a cleaning and a fresh coat, with no sanding. Clear film finishes like varnish and spar urethane tend to crack and peel in the sun. Indoors, either works: a water-based clear coat keeps Sugi close to its natural color, while oils give a richer, darker look.

What's the best finish for a cedar fence?

A penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain on all sides and cut ends, applied before building if you can. It soaks in, shows the grain, and is renewed every few years by cleaning and recoating with no sanding. Or leave it unfinished and let it weather to gray.

Can I leave Sugi unfinished?

Yes. Indoors in dry rooms, unfinished Sugi is common and you can add a finish later. Outdoors, unfinished Sugi will weather to a gray or silver color over time. Keep it clean, and a weathering stain can even out the gray.

Does a clear finish keep Sugi from changing color?

No. All wood changes color with light, finished or not. A clear finish with UV blockers can slow the change near sunny windows, but it can't stop it. Oils and oil-based clears also darken Sugi right away, so test any finish on a spare piece first.

How often do I need to refinish outdoor Sugi?

It depends on the finish and how much sun it gets. Penetrating oils typically last about 2 to 5 years on walls, semi-transparent stains about 2 to 6, and decks about 1 to 3. Covered soffits and porch ceilings last longer than siding in full sun.

What should I put on Sugi in a sauna?

Inside the hot room, walls and ceilings are usually left bare. For benches, use only a sauna oil or paraffin oil made for sauna benches. Skip varnish, polyurethane, lacquer, paint and regular stains, which can soften or give off fumes in the heat. The outside of a sauna needs an exterior finish.

Is shou sugi ban the same as Sugi?

Shou sugi ban (yakisugi) is Sugi or other wood with a charred surface. It's one way to finish Sugi outdoors. The char layer can last for decades with gentle cleaning only, and you should never sand or power wash it.
